# The renewal play

The retention radar exists to run one play well, every week. This page is the play. It is what the manager does after the page tells them who.

## Why renewals

An unhappy property owner rarely leaves over the thing they complained about. They leave when the next bad moment arrives: a vacancy that drags, a repair bill, a confusing statement. Calling them to revisit the old complaint produces a bad call and often the cancellation you were trying to avoid. The lever every company has, at scale and on a calendar, is the lease renewal: a quarter of the book renews inside any 90-day window. A renewal done well is a win you can bring to the owner, unprompted. That is the play: find the owners who are unhappy or about to be, lock the renewal on their property, then reach out with the win.

## The weekly routine

1. Open the radar. The five at the top are this week's work, in order: detractors with a vacancy today, detractors with a renewal inside 90 days, then likely detractors.
2. For a **vacancy today**: this one cannot wait for the renewal. Post the home if it is rent-ready and not posted, set a lease-by date, and reach the owner this week with the leasing plan. Lead with what is being done, not with an apology.
3. For a **renewal inside 90 days**: run the renewal first. Decide the rent (in line with market: renew at the current rent; below market: propose an increase toward market inside the company's cap; above market: renew flat rather than risk a turn), get the tenant's yes, then send the owner the win.
4. For **nothing inside 90 days**: hold. They get the monthly owner update like everyone else; the play runs at their next renewal.
5. Run the same renewal step for every renewal in the window, not only the flagged owners. The owner who is happy today stays happy when the renewal lands with a note.

## The owner note after a renewal

Short, specific, positive. Never mention the survey. Never mention the old complaint unless the owner raises it.

> Hi {first name},
>
> Quick update on {address}: {tenant first name or "your tenant"} renewed for another {term}, at {rent} ({in line with market / up $X from last year / held flat; here is why}). No vacancy, no turn cost, and the home keeps paying through {month}.
>
> Thanks for trusting us with it. If anything about {address} is on your mind, reply here or call me at {direct phone}.
>
> {Manager}

Variants: a renewal held flat on purpose ("the market softened; we recommended holding the rent and keeping a paying tenant, and they signed"), a renewal with an increase ("we proposed $X, in line with the comps, and they accepted"), a vacancy update ("the home is posted at $X, three showings this week, lease-by date the 20th; I'll send the showing report every Friday").

## Prompts the manager asks Claude after reading the page

- "Draft the renewal-win note to {owner} for {address}; the tenant renewed at {rent} for 12 months."
- "Prep me on {owner}: what is happening across their properties, what they said in the survey, and what to lead with."
- "Which of this week's five has a rent-ready home that is not posted?"
- "Rebuild the radar with this month's exports and tell me what changed since last run."

The page does not hold notes or statuses. The manager's CRM, task list or email thread does. Export the list when the task system needs it.

## What a company sees after a few cycles

Companies running the play on every renewal can read their own uplift on the next survey cycle: detractor count, NPS, renewal rate, and the share of renewals that produced an owner note. PropertyManagement.com's benchmark rows on the page show where the company stands against verified property managers at each cycle.
